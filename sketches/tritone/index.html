Two chords that share no notes.

A C drone underneath. Above it, a slow crossfade between D major and A♭ major, two chords a tritone apart. When one fades into the other, every note above the drone changes at once. Halfway, the fade stops for a while so you can hear all six notes together.

Idea and chord choice: Thaura.

The sound comes from AMY, a small open-source synthesiser that also runs on microcontrollers. The page downloads about 5 MB the first time.

02

why these two chords

Move a major chord up six semitones, a tritone, and the new chord has none of the old chord's notes. Nothing carries over from one to the other, so the colour of the whole sound changes at once. Chords a third apart would share a note, and that shared note would soften the change.

There are six pairs like this. Over a C drone, the question is whether either chord has a note that leans somewhere. Three intervals do: a flat seventh turns C into a chord that wants to move to F; a major seventh pulls up to C; a flat ninth grinds against it.

On the clock, an arrow after an interval shows where that note leans: →F or →C.

D and A♭ is the only pair where nothing leans. Over C, D major gives the bright Lydian colour: the 9th, the sharp 11th and the 13th. A♭ major gives the dark minor colour: the flat 6th, the root and the flat 3rd. So the fade moves from about as bright as you can get over C to about as dark, and no note asks to resolve.

halfway

Halfway through the fade you hear C, D, E♭, F♯, A♭ and A. Four of those, C, E♭, F♯ and A, make a diminished seventh chord, built entirely from minor thirds. It is perfectly symmetrical, so it has no home key. Two of its notes come from each chord. On the clock, the dashed lines join each note to its tritone partner. They are darkest halfway, where the two chords become one balanced sound.

the other pair

E and B♭ is there for comparison. The B♭ makes the drone sound like a chord about to move to F, and the B in the E chord leans back up to C. It sounds vast and uneasy, as if it's about to go somewhere.

03

how it's made

AMY takes short text messages. Each one sets up an oscillator or changes it. For example, v106w0n36a0.16l1Z means: oscillator 106, sine wave, note 36 (the low C), level 0.16, start playing.

  • The drone is two slightly detuned sawtooth waves through a slowly moving low-pass filter, with sine waves on C2 and C3.
  • Each chord note is two detuned sawtooth waves and a sine wave. Each note's filter drifts at its own slow rate.
  • The fade is equal-power. One chord's level follows a cosine curve and the other's a sine curve, so the total loudness stays steady. Halfway, each chord is 3 dB down.
  • Every twentieth of a second, the page works out where the fade has reached and sends each oscillator its new level. AMY smooths each change, so you don't hear steps.

The same messages would work wherever AMY runs, including on small boards, so the browser here stands in for hardware. AMY is by Brian Whitman and Daniel PW Ellis, under the MIT licence (licence text).
