slide 02

Design is speculation

on the slide

  • Usability studies are small and narrow in scope (Nielsen 2000; Schmettow 2012)

  • We cannot fully anticipate how an artefact will be used until it is in use (Maceli & Atwood 2011)

How do blind and visually impaired sound creatives achieve access to DAWs, and what should design practice learn from this?

script

Design is speculation. Usability studies are typically small and narrow in scope, as Nielsen and Schmettow discuss, so an hour of testing can't reveal how a product will settle into a complex ecology of people, places and things. Following Maceli and Atwood's vision of a fourth wave of HCI, we cannot fully anticipate how an artefact will be used until it is in use, and requirements keep evolving after release.

So our question: how do blind and visually impaired sound creatives achieve access to digital audio workstations, or DAWs, the software used to record and produce music? And what should design practice learn from this?

slide 03

Method

on the slide

  1. 20 blind and visually impaired sound creatives in 9 countries, recruited through online communities

  2. Two-hour remote sessions: semi-structured interview, then contextual enquiry on a task each participant chose

  3. Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke 2021), led by three authors, one visually impaired, using screen-reader-accessible tools

script

Our method. We worked with twenty blind and visually impaired sound creatives, from nine countries, recruited through online communities such as Reapers without Peepers. Participants were producers, composers, engineers and sound designers, from beginner to advanced.

Each two-hour remote session began with a semi-structured interview, followed by a contextual enquiry, in which participants carried out a task of their choosing, such as mixing audio stems, while we asked about their actions.

We used reflexive thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke. Three authors coded the transcripts, one of whom, James, is visually impaired, so we chose screen-reader-accessible tools for every stage of the analysis.

slide 04

Three gaps

on the slide

"There is a lot of goodwill without the backup. Educators just don't know what's feasible"

Peter Bosher, on educators

script

The analysis identified three gaps. The figure shows them as a path broken at education, information and technology, each spanned by a bridge built by disabled communities.

The education gap: educators often lack awareness of non-visual workflows. As Peter Bosher put it, "There is a lot of goodwill without the backup. Educators just don't know what's feasible"

The information gap: music technology manufacturers give very little information on accessible workflows, so people sometimes buy music software without knowing whether it will work with a screen reader.

The technology gap: interfaces, tutorials and manuals are designed for a normative, sighted user, and describe workflows through visual cues.

slide 05

Communities of practice

on the slide

  • Knowledge is co-constituted and kept current

  • Peripheral participation counts (Lave & Wenger 1991)

  • Ad hoc channels: mailing lists, WhatsApp voice notes

  • Members mentor, advocate and build tools (OSARA)

"The community in general has been very positive on my workflow, because there’ve been numerous things that have changed the way I work now because of a workaround or a little demo someone’s (showed) me"

Rob Williams

script

Online communities of practice, the slide's heading, bridge these gaps. Their knowledge is co-constituted: members pool experiences of products and keep that knowledge current through use. Following Lave and Wenger, membership includes peripheral participation; some members might rarely post, but still learn from the community. Channels are ad hoc and needs-based, such as mailing lists and WhatsApp voice notes, rather than repositories. And members mentor one another, advocate to manufacturers and build tools, such as OSARA, the extension that makes the Reaper DAW usable with a screen reader.

As Rob Williams said: "The community in general has been very positive on my workflow, because there’ve been numerous things that have changed the way I work now because of a workaround or a little demo someone’s (showed) me"

slide 06

A DAW is often accessible only with the community's knowledge and labour.

on the slide

A DAW is often accessible only with the community's knowledge and labour.

Access is interdependent (Bennett et al. 2018) and access work largely invisible (Branham & Kane 2015). Sighted users never have to do this work.

script

This leads to a central claim. A DAW is often accessible only with the disabled community's knowledge and labour.

Following Bennett, Brady and Branham, access here is interdependent: it is co-created by users, communities and, sometimes, manufacturers. And, as Branham and Kane found in workplaces, this access work is largely invisible. OSARA's developers are unpaid volunteers, and Cockos, Reaper's maker, benefits from their labour. Sighted users never have to do this work: they receive a functional product at the point of purchase.

slide 07

Illich: Tools for Conviviality

on the slide

Convivial tools (Priavolou & Niaros 2019) are:

  1. Learnable through use

  2. Used when and where you choose

  3. Adaptable to you

…and rooted in community.

Photo: AP, 1969. Public domain (US), Wikimedia Commons

script

To theorise this, we draw on Ivan Illich's Tools for Conviviality. The slide shows an Associated Press photo of Illich, from 1969, on the telephone.

Illich argues that tools pass a second watershed, where they begin to constrain rather than liberate. The digital studio lowered the cost of recording, but it is far less tangible than its analogue predecessor, and making it accessible demands specialist skill and access labour.

His alternative is the convivial tool. Priavolou and Niaros distil three qualities: it is learnable through use; you decide when and where to use it; and it adapts to you. Crucially, conviviality is rooted in community.

slide 08

Convivial Accessibility Design Guidelines

on the slide

Guideline 1

Build accessible ecosystems

Tutorials and manuals without visual cues

Guideline 2

Embrace interdependent access

Recognise, credit and signpost community work

Guideline 3

Design for designers

Open extension points, as with Reaper's SDK

script

From this synthesis, we propose three Convivial Accessibility Design Guidelines, shown as three cards.

First, build accessible ecosystems: the materials that support a tool, such as tutorials and manuals, must be accessible too, and avoid visual cues.

Second, embrace interdependent access: recognise that designers, disabled or not, don't have all the answers, and credit and signpost the disabled community's access work, for example on product websites.

Third, design for designers: treat disabled people as designers, not only users. Reaper shows that low-level extension points, through its software development kit, can coexist with proprietary software. As we put it in the paper, the DAW should be ajar.

slide 09

Tools must be malleable in use, and communities supported to change them.

on the slide

Tools must be malleable in use, and communities supported to change them.

WCAG is necessary but not sufficient. "Nothing about us without us", where us is an interdependent community of practice.

script

To conclude: tools must be malleable in use, and the communities that change them must be supported. We cannot rely solely on accessibility requirements such as WCAG; we need the adage ‘nothing about us without us’ to permeate both design and development, emphasising the pronoun us, as a community of practice.
